story:
- Students at Cornell University were asked to put on a T-shirt with a large picture of the singer Barry Manilow, chosen because students at the time found it embarrassing. Then each one walked into a room where other students were sitting at a table.
- Soon after, the wearer was asked to estimate how many people in the room could say whose face was on the shirt. Then the researchers asked the people in the room.

q: What percentage of the people in the room could say whose face was on the shirt?
you_real: The real wearers guessed that about half the room had noticed. In fact, only about 23% could say who was on the shirt.
original: The wearers estimated that about 46% of the people in the room would remember the face on the shirt. About 23% actually did. In a second study, with shirts showing people that students liked, the wearers again overestimated how many people noticed.
today: 'The authors called this the “spotlight effect”: we think other people notice our looks and our slips much more than they do. In the same paper, people who had taken part in a group discussion also overestimated how much the others had noticed what they said.'
surprise: Wearers who waited 15 minutes before guessing, and had got used to the shirt, guessed lower.
